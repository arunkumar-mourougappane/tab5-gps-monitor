Six needles for
a crowded sky

The bezel is settled, so it is identical in all six — only the needle changes. What makes this different from an aircraft HSI is that the needle here lies on top of the readout: every pixel of it hides sky. Each panel counts, live, how many satellites its needle is currently sitting on. Turn the sky density up and watch that number, not just the shape.

How to judge these

Three questions

What does it hide?

An aircraft HSI needle points at a bezel; this one points across a data plot. The counter on each panel is the number of satellite dots whose centres fall under solid needle fill — outline and hairline treatments read as zero because you can still see through them.

Which end is forward?

Today's needle is a plain line with a dot at the pivot, so direction is inferred rather than shown. Anything with an asymmetric head answers it instantly; a symmetric diamond does not, unless the halves differ.

What does parked look like?

Course over ground is meaningless below about 1 km/h, so every option needs a resting state. The simulation stops on its own every so often — watch what each one does when it does. A needle that simply vanishes reads as a fault.

Cost is not a factor here

All six are a handful of lines and polygons drawn per frame over the pre-rendered bezel sprite. Pick on legibility, not on cycles — the difference between the cheapest and the dearest of these is nothing next to the blit that carries them.

Bezel geometry as settled: radius 148 inside a 366 px sprite, 10° minor and 30° major ticks, cardinals at 14 px, fixed index at north, elevation rings labelled 30°/60°. Satellite positions use the firmware's projection, rr = radius × (90 − el) / 90, driven by one simulated receiver shared by all six panels. Colours are the rgb565 constants from src/main.cpp.
